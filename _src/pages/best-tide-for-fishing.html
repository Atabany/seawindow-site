There is no single best tide for fishing, but the usual rule of thumb is to fish moving water: the incoming (flood) or outgoing (ebb) tide, rather than slack water, when the current stops. Which direction works better depends on the spot, so treat the tide as one input alongside light, weather and local knowledge.

Moving water vs slack

NOAA defines a flood current as the movement of water toward the shore or up a tidal river or estuary, and an ebb current as the movement away from shore or down the river. Slack water is when the current’s speed is near zero, especially the moment it changes direction. In NOAA’s description of a reversing current, speed builds from zero at slack to a maximum about midway between the slacks.

The usual reasoning is that current carries bait and stirs the water, so trips are planned around the hours of moving water, with slack used to move spots or re-rig. It is a rule of thumb, not a law: your own records from one spot will tell you more than any general rule.

Slack water is not always at high or low tide

Many people assume slack water comes at high and low tide, with the strongest current halfway between. NOAA says this rule of thumb “does not hold for most locations”. Where the tide behaves as a standing wave, slack comes near high and low tide; where it behaves as a progressive wave, often at the ocean entrance to bays and harbors, the strongest current comes around high and low tide and slack falls in between. NOAA’s conclusion: the relationship “is unique to each location and cannot be determined from a generic ‘rule of thumb’.” For current timing, use NOAA’s tidal current predictions.

Spring and neap: how much the water moves

A bigger tide range means more water has to move in the same six hours or so. NOAA notes that tidal currents are strong around new and full moon (spring currents) and weak around the quarter moons (neap currents). At Charleston, NOAA predicts a daily tide range of 4.2 ft on December 16, 2026, a neap day, with currents off Fort Sumter peaking at 1.8 knots; on December 26, just after the full moon, the range is 7.3 ft and the current reaches 3.1 knots. See spring and neap tides for the dates.

Inlets, flats, jetties and surf

  • Inlets and passes. All the water filling or draining a bay goes through them, so current there is often strong and slack short. This is where current predictions matter most.
  • Flats and marsh edges. The question is depth. Some flats are dry or too shallow at low tide; read the tide height, not only the time.
  • Jetties and piers. Current along the rocks changes with the tide stage; slack is often the easiest time to hold bait near the bottom.
  • Surf. The tide moves where waves break over bars and how far the water reaches up the beach. Check waves and swell as well as the tide.

Solunar tables are estimates

Solunar tables mark times when the moon is overhead, underfoot, rising or setting, on the idea that fish feed more then. SeaWindow’s Sky screen shows these as estimated solunar windows: a major period is one hour either side of the moon’s transit, a minor period 30 minutes either side of moonrise or moonset. As the app says, these timings do not predict catches. The tide, light, weather and the water at your spot are better places to start.

FAQ

Is an incoming or outgoing tide better for fishing?

It depends on the spot. Both are moving water. On flats the flood brings back depth; at an inlet the ebb drains the bay through a narrow gap. Your own records from that spot are the best guide.

Is slack tide bad for fishing?

Not always. With little current less water moves past you, but slack can be the easiest time to hold bait near the bottom. It does not always come at high or low tide.

Does the moon affect fishing?

The moon drives the tides, and spring tides move more water. Solunar periods are a separate idea; SeaWindow shows them as estimates that do not predict catches.

Does SeaWindow show tidal currents?

No. It shows tide heights and times. For current speed and slack water, use NOAA’s tidal current predictions.

How do I find the fastest-moving part of the tide?

By the rule of twelfths, the water level rises or falls fastest in the middle two sixths between a low and a high. The tide height calculator works it out from two tides.
