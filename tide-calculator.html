How the rule of twelfths works

A tide does not rise at a steady speed. It starts slowly after low water, runs fastest around mid-tide, and slows again as it approaches high water. The rule of twelfths turns that shape into numbers you can work out in your head.

  1. Find the range. Subtract the low-tide height from the high-tide height. A low of 0.9 ft and a high of 5.9 ft give a range of 5.0 ft.
  2. Find the tide’s “hour”. Divide the time between the two tides by six. The rule is named for six-hour tides, but most tides last a little more: 6 h 48 min gives six parts of 68 minutes.
  3. Add the twelfths. After one part the water has moved 1/12 of the range, after two parts 3/12, after three 6/12 (half), after four 9/12, after five 11/12, and after six the full range.
  4. Add the result to the starting height on a rising tide, or subtract it on a falling tide.

Halfway between low and high, the water is at half the range. That is the one moment the rule and the real curve always agree.

The cosine method

Between a low and a high, the tide curve is close to half of a cosine wave. If the tide takes T minutes to go from one extreme to the next, the share of the range covered after t minutes is:

fraction = (1 − cos(π × t ÷ T)) ÷ 2

Multiply the fraction by the range and add it to the first tide’s height (or subtract it on a falling tide). To find when the water reaches a height h, turn the formula round: t = T × arccos(1 − 2 × (h − first height) ÷ (second height − first height)) ÷ π. The calculator does both, for the cosine curve and for the rule of twelfths.

NOAA computes its hourly heights from the full set of tidal constituents for the station, so its curve is not exactly symmetrical; the estimates are. Results like this are typical of a simple semidiurnal tide on the open coast, and can be much worse elsewhere.

When the estimate goes wrong

  • Unusual tide shapes. Some places have a double high water or a long stand, such as Southampton in England, or tides distorted in shallow estuaries. Neither method fits them.
  • Mixed and diurnal tides. On coasts with one tide a day, or two very unequal tides, use the actual high and low on either side of your time, not a nominal six hours.
  • Weather. Wind, air pressure and river flow move the water away from any prediction, by a foot or more in a storm. The estimate only interpolates the prediction you entered.
  • The wrong station. The result is only as good as the two tides you enter. A station far from your beach can be off by many minutes.

For depth under a boat, use official charts and the method your training or pilot book uses for that harbour. This calculator is a planning aid, not a navigation tool.

FAQ

What is the rule of twelfths?

A rule of thumb for estimating tide height between a low and a high. Divide the time between them into six equal parts; the water moves 1/12, 2/12, 3/12, 3/12, 2/12 and 1/12 of the tidal range in those parts, so it is a quarter of the way after two parts and halfway after three.

Is the rule of twelfths accurate?

On a regular semidiurnal tide it is usually within a few percent of the range. In the San Francisco example on this page it was within 0.16 ft of NOAA’s hourly predictions. It fails where the tide has an unusual shape, such as a double high water, and it never includes weather.

What is the difference between the rule of twelfths and the cosine method?

The rule of twelfths is a stepwise approximation you can do in your head. The cosine method uses the smooth half-cosine shape of a tide between two extremes. They agree exactly after two, three and four sixths of the time and differ by at most about 2.6% of the range.

Can I use this calculator for any tide station?

Yes. Enter two consecutive tides (a high and a low) from any tide table, in feet or metres. Use the station closest to where you will be, and remember that both methods assume a regular tide shape.

Does the calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser. The share link only puts your numbers in the web address.
